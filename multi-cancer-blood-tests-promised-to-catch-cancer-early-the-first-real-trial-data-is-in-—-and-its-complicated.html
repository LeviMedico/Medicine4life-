Diagnostics

Multi-cancer blood tests promised to catch cancer early. The first real trial data is in — and it's complicated.

Sushil Dethaliya · July 18, 2026

For years, the pitch behind multi-cancer early detection (MCED) tests has sounded almost too good: one blood draw, screening for dozens of cancer types at once, catching disease before symptoms even start. The best-known version, GRAIL's Galleri test, has been used by tens of thousands of people already. This year, the first results from the trial actually designed to test whether it works arrived — and the honest answer is: partly, in a more complicated way than the pitch suggested.

How it's supposed to work

Galleri and tests like it look for tiny fragments of DNA that tumors shed into the bloodstream, along with patterns of chemical modifications on that DNA that differ between cancerous and healthy cells. If the test finds a signal, it also tries to predict where in the body the cancer is likely coming from, so doctors know where to look next. It's designed for the roughly 70% of cancer deaths that come from cancer types with no standard screening test at all — there's no routine blood test for pancreatic or ovarian cancer the way there is a mammogram for breast cancer.

What the trial actually found

The NHS-Galleri trial is the first large, randomized, controlled trial of an MCED test — 140,000 participants, the gold standard of medical evidence. Its main goal was to see whether annual Galleri screening would reduce the number of cancers caught late, at stage III or IV, when treatment options narrow and outcomes worsen.

It didn't hit that goal. Overall late-stage cancer rates weren't significantly lower in the screened group.

A trial can miss its main goal and still teach something real — this is one of those cases.

But the results weren't nothing, either. Screening did shift some diagnoses earlier within that late-stage window — fewer cancers were caught at stage IV specifically, the most advanced and hardest to treat point. Screened participants also had fewer cancers discovered through emergency room visits or once symptoms had already appeared, which tend to be more advanced and harder to treat than anything caught by a scheduled screening. Separately, an even larger study — GRAIL's PATHFINDER 2, with nearly 36,000 participants — found that adding Galleri to standard screening (mammograms, colonoscopies, and so on) increased the total number of cancers detected 6.5-fold.

Why the results read as mixed, not as a failure or a win

Both things are true at once: the test didn't do the one specific job the trial was designed to prove (cutting late-stage diagnoses overall), and it did show real signals of benefit in more specific ways (shifting stage IV diagnoses earlier, cutting emergency-setting diagnoses, and finding more cancers overall when added to existing screening). Researchers involved in the trial have been careful to frame it as a genuine, informative result rather than a simple pass or fail — there's real data to learn from either way, not just a headline verdict.

What's still missing is the outcome that matters most in the end: whether any of this actually helps people live longer. That requires longer follow-up than a 3-year trial can show, and it's the next thing researchers are watching for.

Where things stand right now

GRAIL completed its FDA approval application for Galleri in January 2026, and the test already carries the FDA's "Breakthrough Device" designation, which speeds up review for technologies addressing serious unmet needs. That doesn't mean approval is guaranteed or imminent — it means the review process is actively underway. In the UK, the NHS has said it will wait for the trial's fuller results before deciding whether to roll out MCED screening nationally.

For now, Galleri remains available in the US only as an elective, out-of-pocket test — not yet an FDA-approved or insurance-covered screening tool, and not a replacement for existing recommended screenings like mammograms or colonoscopies. The honest takeaway from this year's data: multi-cancer blood tests are a real, promising direction — just not yet the settled, proven tool the early marketing sometimes implied.

Sources: ASCO 2026 Annual Meeting presentations (NHS-Galleri trial, PATHFINDER 2 study); GRAIL press releases; Cancer Therapy Advisor; Clinical Lab Products.
