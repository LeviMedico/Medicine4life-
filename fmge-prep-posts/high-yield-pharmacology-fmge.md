Pharmacology can feel like an impossibly large subject to revise close to the exam — hundreds of drugs, each with its own mechanism, side effects, and contraindications. The good news: FMGE pharmacology questions repeat certain patterns far more than they test obscure facts, and recognizing those patterns is worth more than trying to memorize every drug in the formulary.

## The pattern FMGE relies on most: mechanism-to-side-effect logic

A large share of pharmacology questions aren't really asking "what is this drug's side effect" — they're testing whether you can reason from mechanism to consequence. If you know a drug blocks a specific receptor or enzyme, you can usually predict its major side effect even without memorizing it directly. This is why understanding *why* a side effect happens is more efficient prep than memorizing side-effect lists drug by drug.

## Drug classes that repeat most often

- **Antimicrobials** — mechanism of action grouped by class (cell wall vs. protein synthesis vs. DNA/RNA inhibitors), and which specific side effect or contraindication is classically tested for each (e.g., which antibiotic is avoided in pregnancy, which one requires monitoring for ototoxicity)
- **Anti-hypertensives and cardiac drugs** — especially drug-of-choice questions in specific clinical scenarios (which antihypertensive in pregnancy, which one in a patient with asthma, which one post-MI)
- **NSAIDs and analgesics** — mechanism-based side effects (GI bleeding, renal effects) and which NSAID is preferred or avoided in specific situations
- **Anti-epileptics and psychiatric drugs** — drug-of-choice for specific seizure types, and signature side effects unique to a single drug in the class (these unique identifiers are common exam bait)
- **Drugs in pregnancy and lactation** — a recurring theme across almost every drug class, so it's worth learning as its own cross-cutting topic rather than only within each class

<p class="pull">If a drug has one truly unique, unforgettable side effect, that's almost always the one FMGE tests — not its common, shared side effects.</p>

## A practical way to use limited time

Rather than revising drug classes exhaustively, go through recent past-paper questions first and note which specific facts about each drug were actually asked. This "reverse-engineered" high-yield list, built from real repeated questions, is usually a shorter and more efficient revision path than working through a full pharmacology textbook again from the top.

## The bottom line

Pharmacology rewards focused recognition of drug-specific "signature" facts (unique side effects, unique contraindications, drug-of-choice scenarios) far more than broad memorization. Prioritize accordingly with the time you have left.
