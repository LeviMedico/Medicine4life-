How to actually read a clinical trial paper

Medicine4life · July 16, 2026

Every article on this site — the pancreatic cancer drug, the pharmacogenomics piece — is ultimately built on a clinical trial paper. If you're heading into clinical practice, you'll be reading these for the rest of your career, whether it's to counsel a patient, choose between treatments, or just keep up with your field. Here's how to actually get through one without just skimming the abstract and trusting the headline.

Start with the study design, not the results

Before looking at any numbers, figure out what kind of trial this is. A randomized controlled trial (RCT) is the strongest design — patients are randomly assigned to treatment or control, which balances out both known and unknown confounding factors. An observational study just watches what happens without assigning treatment, which is weaker evidence, though still useful when RCTs aren't ethical or possible.

Also check the phase. Phase 1 is about safety in a small group. Phase 2 checks effectiveness and dosing. Phase 3 is the large-scale, comparative trial that's typically required before approval — this is the one that actually tells you how a treatment performs against a real alternative, in a large enough group to trust the result.

The p-value tells you less than people think

A p-value under 0.05 is usually called "statistically significant," but that number alone doesn't tell you how big or how meaningful the effect is — just that it's unlikely to be due to random chance alone, assuming the study was well-designed. A tiny, clinically irrelevant difference can still hit p < 0.05 if the sample size is large enough.

Statistically significant and clinically significant are not the same question — always ask both.

Confidence intervals matter more than most students think

A 95% confidence interval gives you a plausible range for the true effect size, not just a single point estimate. A drug that reduces risk by "20%, 95% CI: 2%–38%" is a much shakier finding than one that shows "20%, 95% CI: 15%–25%" — even though both papers might report the same headline number. Wide intervals usually mean a smaller sample size or more variability in the data, and that changes how much you should trust the finding.

Check what they're actually measuring

Look closely at the primary endpoint — the main outcome the trial was designed to detect. A cancer drug trial might report overall survival, or it might report progression-free survival, which is a different (and often less convincing) thing. Trials sometimes report multiple secondary endpoints too; if the primary endpoint didn't hit significance but a secondary one did, treat that finding cautiously; it's usually a weaker, hypothesis-generating result rather than a confirmed one.

Who was actually studied?

Check the inclusion and exclusion criteria. A drug tested only in otherwise-healthy 40-year-olds tells you less about how it'll perform in your 75-year-old patient with three comorbidities. This is one of the most common gaps between "what the trial showed" and "what it means for the patient in front of you."

A quick mental checklist

  • What kind of study is this, and what phase?
  • What's the primary endpoint, and did it actually hit significance?
  • How wide is the confidence interval?
  • Who was studied, and does that population look like your patient?
  • Who funded it, and could that create bias?

None of this requires being a statistician. It just means slowing down before turning a headline into a clinical opinion — a habit that pays off every single time you read a new drug trial for the rest of your career.
