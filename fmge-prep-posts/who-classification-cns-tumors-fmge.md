The WHO Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System, now in its fifth edition (WHO CNS5, 2021), is dense enough to intimidate anyone trying to revise it close to an exam. The good news for FMGE specifically: the exam tends to test a fairly narrow, predictable slice of it, rather than the full depth pathologists actually use in practice.

## The big conceptual shift worth understanding first

Before memorizing categories, it's worth understanding the one idea that changed everything in this edition: molecular and genetic markers now often outrank pure histology in determining a tumor's classification. The clearest example is IDH mutation status, which is now central to how gliomas are classified and named. Grading also switched from Roman numerals (I–IV) to Arabic numerals (1–4), applied more consistently across tumor types than in the previous edition. If a question tests "what changed" rather than "what is," this is almost always the answer.

## The 12 major categories, and which ones actually get tested

1. **Gliomas, glioneuronal tumors, and neuronal tumors** — by far the highest-yield category. Know that adult-type diffuse gliomas are now separated by IDH mutation status (astrocytoma IDH-mutant vs. oligodendroglioma IDH-mutant/1p19q-codeleted vs. glioblastoma IDH-wildtype), and that pediatric gliomas are now classified as their own separate low-grade and high-grade categories rather than graded the same way as adult tumors.
2. **Choroid plexus tumors** — lower yield, mostly just needs recognition.
3. **Embryonal tumors** — medulloblastoma is a classic FMGE topic; know that it's now split into molecular subgroups (WNT-activated, SHH-activated, non-WNT/non-SHH), and that WNT-activated medulloblastoma classically has the best prognosis of the group.
4. **Pineal tumors** — low yield for FMGE specifically.
5. **Cranial and paraspinal nerve tumors** — schwannoma and neurofibroma are worth knowing at a definition level; MPNST is a common distractor option.
6. **Meningiomas** — a genuinely important change: meningioma is now treated as a single tumor type with grades 1 to 3, rather than the long list of separate histological subtypes from before. This unification itself is a common "what changed" question.
7–12. **Mesenchymal tumors, melanocytic tumors, hematolymphoid tumors, germ cell tumors, sellar region tumors, and metastases** — lower individual yield, but two specific facts repeat: primary CNS lymphoma as the main hematolymphoid entity, and the renaming of "pituitary adenoma" to "pituitary neuroendocrine tumor (PitNET)" as the now-preferred term.

<p class="pull">FMGE rarely asks you to reproduce the full classification — it asks whether you know what changed, and why one specific tumor now gets classified differently than it used to.</p>

## A practical way to revise this topic

Don't try to memorize the entire classification tree. Instead, learn it as a short list of "what changed and why" facts: IDH status splitting gliomas, medulloblastoma's molecular subgroups, meningioma's unification into one type with three grades, and the PitNET renaming. These four changes account for a disproportionate share of the CNS-tumor-classification questions that actually show up, because they're the parts of the new edition most different from what older, more widely available study material still teaches.

## The bottom line

Treat this topic the way you'd treat a "recent update" question in any subject: know what changed from the previous system, and why, rather than trying to hold the entire dense classification in memory. That narrower, targeted approach covers most of what FMGE is actually likely to ask.
