What "number needed to treat" actually tells you

Sushil Dethaliya · July 18, 2026

If you've read our earlier piece on how to actually read a clinical trial paper, you already know that a p-value alone doesn't tell you how big an effect is. Number needed to treat, or NNT, is one of the best tools for closing that gap — and it's one of the most underused numbers in how research gets talked about, both in classrooms and in the news.

What it actually is

NNT answers a simple, practical question: how many patients need to receive a treatment for one additional patient to benefit, compared to not receiving it? If a drug has an NNT of 10, you'd need to treat 10 people with it to prevent one heart attack, stroke, or whatever the outcome being measured is — beyond what would have happened anyway.

Lower NNT means a more powerfully effective treatment. An NNT of 2 is remarkable — most patients benefit. An NNT of 100 means the treatment helps, but you're treating a lot of people to prevent one bad outcome in one of them.

Why this number gets hidden behind flashier ones

Drug trials and news coverage almost always lead with relative risk reduction, because it's the most impressive-sounding number available. "Cuts risk by 50%" sounds dramatic — but 50% of what? If a condition's baseline risk is already very low, cutting it in half might mean going from a 2% chance to a 1% chance. That's a real, valid effect — but it also means treating 100 people to help just one of them, which an NNT of 100 makes immediately obvious in a way "50% risk reduction" does not.

Relative risk reduction tells you how impressive a number sounds. NNT tells you how many people you're actually treating to help one of them.

This isn't a criticism of relative risk as a statistic — it's a legitimate and useful measure. The problem is relying on it alone, without ever translating it into what it means at the patient level.

A worked example

Say a drug reduces the risk of a stroke from 4% to 2% over five years. That's a 50% relative risk reduction — a genuinely accurate, impressive-sounding claim. But the absolute risk reduction is just 2 percentage points, which gives an NNT of 50. You'd need to treat 50 people with this drug for five years to prevent one stroke. The other 49 people take the medication, deal with any side effects and cost, and get no measurable benefit from it, at least for this specific outcome.

None of that means the drug isn't worth taking — for many conditions, an NNT of 50 is still a reasonable trade-off, especially for a serious outcome like stroke. But it's a very different mental picture than "cuts your risk in half."

The companion number: NNH

Number needed to harm (NNH) works the same way, but for a side effect instead of a benefit — how many patients need to take a drug for one additional patient to experience a specific harm. A treatment with a low NNT and a high NNH is a strong case. A treatment with a similar NNT and NNH is a much harder call, and exactly the kind of trade-off you'll be weighing with real patients.

How to actually use this

Next time you see a headline drug statistic, or a trial's abstract leading with a relative risk figure, try converting it in your head: what's the baseline risk, what's the actual absolute difference, and roughly how many people would need this treatment for one of them to benefit? You don't need the exact NNT calculation on the spot — just the habit of asking the question before deciding a treatment sounds impressive or not.

It's a small habit that changes how you read every drug trial, every FDA approval headline, and eventually, every conversation you have with a patient about whether a medication is actually worth taking.
