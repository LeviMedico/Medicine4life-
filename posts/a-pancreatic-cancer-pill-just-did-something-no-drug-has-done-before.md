Pancreatic cancer has a reputation doctors don't sugarcoat: by the time most people are diagnosed, the disease has usually spread, and treatment options are limited. A new pill just changed one of the most stubborn numbers in oncology — how long patients actually survive.
The drug, daraxonrasib, comes from Revolution Medicines and works differently from standard chemotherapy. Instead of broadly attacking fast-dividing cells, it targets RAS — a family of proteins that, when mutated, act like a stuck accelerator pedal telling cancer cells to keep growing. Most pancreatic tumors carry a RAS mutation, which is part of why the disease is so hard to treat and why a drug built specifically to block it is a meaningful departure from business as usual.

### ***What the trial actually found***

In a global Phase 3 trial of patients whose pancreatic cancer had already progressed past a first round of chemotherapy, daraxonrasib nearly doubled how long people lived. Patients taking the daily pill had a median survival of 13.2 months, compared to 6.7 months for those on standard intravenous chemotherapy — and cut the relative risk of death by 60%. 


***No pancreatic cancer drug has ever shown a survival benefit over a full year in a late-stage trial. This one just did.


Researchers involved in the trial described the results as "practice-changing" — language that doesn't get used loosely in oncology, where incremental gains of a few weeks are often what counts as progress.***

### ***Why this matters beyond the headline number***

Pancreatic cancer's five-year survival rate sits around 13%, largely because most cases are caught after the disease has already spread and options run out quickly. A treatment that meaningfully extends survival at that late stage addresses exactly the point where current medicine struggles most.
There's also a second layer here that oncologists are watching closely: daraxonrasib is being tested not just on its own, but in combination with existing chemotherapy drugs in earlier-stage patients, where early data has shown promising tumor responses. Researchers see it less as a one-off drug and more as a foundation — a RAS-targeting approach that could eventually extend to other RAS-driven cancers, which make up a significant share of cancers overall.

## ***What happens next***

Daraxonrasib isn't approved yet. Revolution Medicines is preparing to submit this data to the FDA, aided by a "Priority Voucher" program designed to speed up review for treatments addressing major health needs — but a formal approval and public availability are still ahead, not immediate.
For patients and families dealing with a pancreatic cancer diagnosis today, this isn't a treatment available yet — it's a trial result. But it's a rare kind: a result strong enough that even researchers who study this disease for a living are using words like "unprecedented."
