*Most wearables that touch women's health are add-ons — a period tracker bolted onto a device originally built to count steps and measure sleep. Clair Health, a startup founded by two recent Stanford graduates, is pitching something different: a wrist-worn device built from the ground up around the hormonal cycle, rather than around a generic fitness-tracking chassis. The company just closed an $11.6 million seed round led by Khosla Ventures, with backers including 23andMe co-founder Anne Wojcicki, and plans to ship its first units in November*.

### What the device actually does

Clair's wristband packs in 10 biosensors — more than double what a typical smartwatch carries — including a biomagnetic sensor the company says isn't used in any competing consumer wearable. Instead of measuring hormones directly, the device reads physiological proxies: skin temperature, heart rate variability, and electrodermal activity. An AI model then runs those signals through more than 130 proprietary biomarkers to estimate levels of estrogen, progesterone, LH, and FSH, and to classify which phase of the menstrual cycle a person is likely in. A companion feature also analyzes short voice recordings during onboarding, aiming to pick up additional cycle-related signals from speech patterns.


This is an inference model, not a direct measurement — conceptually closer to how a continuous glucose monitor estimates blood sugar from interstitial fluid rather than drawing blood, than to an actual hormone panel. That's not automatically a flaw; CGMs proved indirect measurement can still be clinically useful. But it does mean the accuracy of Clair's output depends entirely on how well its models generalize across different bodies, cycles, and health conditions — something that's much harder to validate than a chemical assay.

### What the evidence says so far

The company reports 94% accuracy in classifying menstrual cycle phase, benchmarked against daily urine ovulation tests — a reasonable at-home comparison point, but not the same standard as a clinical blood hormone panel. In beta testing, Clair also says it identified nine distinct hormonal sub-phases, roughly double the four-phase model most health education still teaches. That's an intriguing claim, but "identified" here means detected by the company's own models, not confirmed against an external clinical gold standard.


Clair says it's launching an independent trial through Stanford's Gladstone BeeHive program, with a stated intent to publish peer-reviewed results. That trial — not the funding round — is really the thing worth watching. Until independent, published validation exists, the 94% figure and the nine-phase claim should be read as promising internal benchmarks rather than established clinical fact.

### Why it matters

Roughly a third of women have irregular cycles, which the company argues makes calendar-based period-tracking apps unreliable for a large share of users. Hormonal health has also long been underserved by wearable tech: most fitness devices were designed around metrics like heart rate and step count, with women's health features layered on afterward. If continuous, passive hormone monitoring works as described, it could give both patients and clinicians a much richer picture of cycle irregularities, perimenopause transitions, and conditions like PCOS or endometriosis than a single office visit and a patient's memory of symptoms ever could.


The company is explicitly launching Clair as a wellness product rather than a diagnostic one, with FDA clearance planned as a later step — a common path for consumer health wearables, but one that means the device isn't held to the same evidence bar as a regulated diagnostic tool at launch. For now, Clair is a genuinely novel approach to a real gap in women's health tracking — but the gap between "promising sensor data" and "something a doctor can act on" is exactly where independent validation, not funding announcements, does the real work.
